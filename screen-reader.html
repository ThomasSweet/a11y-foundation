A screen reader’s first fifteen minutes

Nothing teaches accessibility faster than hearing your own page through a screen readerScreen readerAssistive technology that speaks the page aloud (VoiceOver, NVDA, JAWS), navigating by the semantics in the markup — the reason markup quality is something you can hear. — and nothing gets postponed harder. This sheet is the on-ramp: the handful of keystrokes that matter, and a plan for the first fifteen minutes.

Two rules before you start

Learn the stop key before anything else. Speech you can’t silence is why most first attempts end inside a minute. It’s Control — on both.

Don’t arrow through the page word by word. That’s the tourist’s walk. People who use screen readers daily pull up the list of headings and jump — WebAIM’s surveys have found heading navigation the most common way to find information for over a decade. Your job isn’t to narrate the page; it’s to check the shortcuts have somewhere to land.

VoiceOver, already on your Mac

Ships with macOS. Pair it with Safari — that’s the combination Apple builds and tests together.

⌘+F5
Turn VoiceOver on or off. On a Touch ID Mac, hold ⌘ and press Touch ID three times.
Control
Stop speech, immediately. Learn this one first.
Control+Option
The VO keys, held at the start of every command below — written as VO. Caps Lock works too.
VO+→
Move to the next item; ← moves back.
VO+Space
Press the thing you are on.
VO+U
Open the rotor — lists of headings, landmarks, links, and form controls. Switch lists with ← and →.
VO+⌘+H
Jump to the next heading.
VO+A
Read continuously from here.

NVDA, free on Windows

Download it from nvaccess.org; it pairs well with both Chrome and Firefox. On web pages it runs in browse mode, which is why single letters below work as jumps — the page is treated as a document, not a text field.

Ctrl+Alt+N
Start NVDA.
Control
Stop speech — same reflex, same key.
Insert
The NVDA key, for commands that need it. Caps Lock can stand in if you chose that at setup.
H
Jump to the next heading; Shift + H goes back.
1–6
Jump to the next heading of exactly that level.
D
Jump to the next landmark.
K
Jump to the next link.
F
Jump to the next form field.
Insert+F7
The elements list — every heading, link, and landmark on the page in one searchable dialog.
Insert+↓
Read continuously from here.

The phone in your pocket

In WebAIM’s eleventh screen-reader survey (2026), 91.8% of screen reader users reported using one on a mobile device. The phone pass is the majority experience, not extra credit. On an iPhone: Settings → Accessibility → VoiceOver, and set the side-button triple-press shortcut while you’re there. On Android the same job is TalkBack.

Swipe →
Move to the next item; swipe ← moves back.
Double-tap
Press the item you just heard.
Two-finger tap
Stop speech.
Two-finger twist
The rotor — choose what swiping up and down steps through, headings included.

Spend the fifteen minutes like this

  1. Minutes 0–3, somewhere you didn’t build. Turn the screen reader on, on a page you have no stake in, and practice the stop key until it’s a reflex. You can’t think while it talks; everyone silences it constantly.
  2. 3–6, the outline. On your own page, pull up the heading list — the rotor on VoiceOver, the elements list on NVDA — and read only that. Does it tell the page’s story in order? That list is your page’s table of contents, and it’s often the first bug report.
  3. 6–9, the landmarks. Jump by landmark. Is there a main? Do repeated navs have names that tell them apart? Then press Tab once from the top of the page: does a skip link surface?
  4. 9–12, the controls. Tab through everything interactive. Each stop should announce a name, a role, and a state. Silence, “clickable”, or “button” with no name are your bugs. Submit a form empty: does focus land somewhere that explains what went wrong?
  5. 12–15, one real task. Pick the page’s main job and finish it with your eyes off the screen. Slow is fine — stuck is a finding.

What fifteen minutes doesn’t prove

It doesn’t make you a screen reader user. People who rely on this software are far faster than you’ll be today and lean on shortcuts you haven’t met — your slowness is yours, not theirs. And passing your own ear test is not the same as being accessible: this catches the loud failures. The quiet ones still need the layered model — and, eventually, feedback from people who use this software every day.

To spend the plan on a page built for it, the listening room is where you can hear all of this: eleven barriers a scanner passes over, every one of them audible before it is visible, answers included.
